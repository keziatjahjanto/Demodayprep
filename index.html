Welcome

This handbook teaches you to present your project in two minutes, so that the people watching remember who it helps and why it matters. It is the written companion to four two-minute videos, and you can use either one on its own.

Two minutes is not long. That is the point. A short demo forces you to choose the one person, the one problem and the one moment that prove your project works. Everything else can wait for questions.

Most demos fall short in the same two ways: they walk through screens instead of telling a story, and they are hard to hear. A strong build deserves better. This handbook is about closing that gap.

A note before you start: you do not need real users to present well. Frontier Commons builds paper airplanes, not rocket ships. A well-drawn example person, which we call a persona, is the right way to show a prototype. Your job is to put that person at the centre of the story.

Chapter 1

Tell it as a story

A demo that tours screens is forgotten by the next presenter. A demo that follows one person through one moment is remembered. This chapter shows you how to turn your project into that story, with your product as its climax.

Start with your “so what”

Before you open a slide or a screen recorder, write one sentence:

[Project] helps [person] [do what] so that [change]. We’d like you to [ask].

For example, for a made-up project: “Borrow Box helps new international students borrow household basics from neighbours, so that nobody spends their first week without what they need. We’d like you to try it in one neighbourhood.”

Then test it. Read your sentence to a partner and ask them to reply, “So what?” Answer, then let them ask again. Do this three times. Your first answer is usually about features. By the third, you are talking about what changes for a person. That third answer is your real “so what”.

If you cannot write this sentence yet, you are not ready to present. That’s fine: it means you have found the most useful thing to work on.

What the research says

In a well-known exercise at Stanford, students gave one-minute persuasive speeches. Most packed two or three statistics into each speech (2.5 on average), but only one in ten told a story. Asked afterwards what they remembered, 63% of listeners recalled the stories and just 5% recalled any single statistic. The most polished speakers were not the most remembered: the ones who told a story, or stressed a single point, were.

The arc: four beats in two minutes

Every demo follows one arc, called the 4 C’s: context, conflict, climax and closure. It is the same shape as a three-act story: the problem, the solution, and what happens next.

Time Beat What it does
0:00–0:15 Context Introduces one person in one moment
0:15–0:35 Conflict Shows why that moment is hard for them today
0:35–1:20 Climax Shows your product solving it, through that person
1:20–2:00 Closure Says what changed for them, what you learned, and your ask

Notice where the demo sits: in the middle, as the climax. It is not the whole presentation. The story before it is what makes the audience care when it arrives.

One person

Give your persona three things:

  • A name. “Amara”, not “a user”.
  • One specific moment. A time, a place and a feeling: “Amara arrived at midnight to an empty flat. No pot, no lamp, no one to ask.”
  • One thing they want. “She wants a warm meal in her own kitchen.”

Keep that person in every beat. If they disappear for thirty seconds while you explain a feature, the story has stopped.

One task

Ask yourself: what does my persona come to do, and on which screen does their problem get solved? That single task is your demo. Every other screen is optional.

When you show it, describe what the person does, not what you click:

Instead of Say
“Click the plus icon, then choose a date.” “Amara borrows a pot from Joe for Saturday.”
“This is the dashboard.” “Here is everything Amara has borrowed this month.”
“There’s also a settings page where you can…” (Leave it out. Save it for questions.)

Give the demo about 45 seconds of your two minutes. Then end where you began: with your person, now better off, and your ask.

Before and after

The same 20 seconds of a made-up project, told two ways.

Before, a walkthrough:

“This is Borrow Box. On the home screen you can see listings. If you click here, you get an item page, and this button sends a request. There’s also a calendar.”

After, a story:

“Amara moved in on Tuesday with one suitcase. Borrow Box shows her Joe, two streets away, who has a pot to lend. She taps Borrow, and on Saturday she cooks her first meal at home.”

The second version shows the same three screens. It just lets Amara use them.

Sketch it as a storyboard

Before you record, sketch your beats as five rough frames, one box per beat. Stick figures are fine. A storyboard shows you at a glance whether one person runs through every frame, and whether your demo is just one box out of five.

Exercise

  1. Write your “so what” sentence. Ask a partner to reply “So what?” three times, and rewrite after each round.
  2. Write five beat cards: context, conflict, climax, what you learned, and closure.
  3. List every screen in your demo. Cross out each one your persona’s task doesn’t need.
  4. Record two different orders of your cards on your phone. Play both to your partner and keep the one they remember better.
  5. Sketch your beats as a five-frame storyboard, using Worksheet 6.

Done when

  • Your “so what” sentence is under 25 words
  • Your persona appears in the first 15 seconds and again in the last 20
  • Your demo takes 45 seconds or less, and every screen is part of one task

Chapter 2

Make it believable

Every audience silently asks one question: would this actually work? Even a paper airplane gets asked it. This chapter gives you two tools to answer it in about twenty seconds: one testing beat and one number you can stand behind.

Assumed, heard, changed

The strongest evidence a prototype can show is that you listened and it changed. Say it in three sentences:

  1. We assumed what you believed when you started.
  2. Testers told us what you heard when real people tried it, even two or three.
  3. So we changed what you did about it.

A made-up example: the Borrow Box team assumed new students wanted cheap basics to buy. Testers told them they only needed things for the first few weeks, until they knew what to keep. So the team changed it to lend instead of sell, with a return date.

That beat takes about fifteen seconds, and it does more for your credibility than any feature. It shows you are learning, not just building.

If you haven’t tested yet, that’s your next step before Demo Day. Show your prototype to three people from your audience and write down one surprise.

One number that holds

A statistic can make your problem feel real, or make you look careless. Use one number, and handle it in three steps:

  1. State it in plain words.
  2. Show the source on screen: who found it, and when.
  3. Say what it proves, and what it doesn’t. “This tells us many new students arrive with little money to spare. It doesn’t tell us our app fixes that; that’s what we’re testing.”

Put the number after you introduce your persona. Then it explains their story, instead of standing alone as a fact the audience has to hold in their head.

What the research says

Why only one number? In a 2007 study, people who read about one girl, Rokia, gave more than twice as much to Save the Children as people who read statistics about hunger in Africa. People who read Rokia’s story plus the statistics gave less than those who read her story alone. Numbers can quietly drown out the person. Use one, put it after your persona, and make it serve their story.

Be honest about the stage

Say plainly what is real and what is not:

  • Your persona is an example, not a real user. Say so once.
  • Name which parts work today and which are planned.
  • If the data on screen is made up for the demo, say so.

This doesn’t weaken your demo. A paper airplane is credible when it doesn’t pretend to be a rocket ship. Trust comes from what you learned, not from how finished it looks.

Exercise

  1. Write your assumed / heard / changed beat in three sentences.
  2. Choose one number. Find its original source and note the date.
  3. Finish this sentence: “This shows ___, but not ___.”

Done when

  • Your script has one testing beat
  • Every number has its source on screen
  • You say clearly what is working and what is planned

Chapter 3

The hook

You have about ten seconds before people decide whether to keep listening. The hook is how you use them. It comes third in this handbook on purpose: you can only write a good opening once you know your story.

Weak and strong openings

Weak Strong
“Hi, I’m Alex, and today I’ll show you my app, which has three features.” “Amara arrived at midnight to an empty flat. No pot, no lamp, no one to ask.”
“So, um, our project is about community.” “Have you ever moved somewhere with nothing but a suitcase?”
“Here’s our home screen.” “What if every new student could borrow what they need in their first week?”

The weak openings talk about you or your app. The strong ones put a person or a problem in the viewer’s head before anything else.

What the research says

Video data backs this up. Wistia, a video platform, found that viewers leave fastest at the very start, before a video has hooked them. Its advice for the opening: get to the point, cut intro graphics, show rather than state your point, and avoid talking about yourself. Shorter videos lose far fewer people in those first moments, one more reason to keep to two minutes.

Four kinds of hook

Choose one. Each takes ten to fifteen seconds.

Hook How it works Example
A moment Drop the viewer into your persona’s scene “It’s 2 a.m. and Amara’s plane just landed.”
A question Ask something the viewer answers in their head “What was the first thing you needed in a new home?”
A surprising fact One number with its source on screen A finding from a named study, with its year
A “what if” Paint the world your product makes possible “What if no student spent their first week without a pot or a lamp?”

A moment is usually the easiest, because it leads straight into your persona’s story from Chapter 1.

Three rules

  1. Start with a person or a problem, not the app.
  2. Give your name after the hook, and the product’s name too.
  3. Keep it under 15 seconds, and let it lead straight into the conflict.

Think of the hook as a promise. The rest of your demo keeps it.

Exercise

  1. Write three hooks for your project, each a different kind.
  2. Read each one aloud to a partner.
  3. An hour later, ask which one they remember. Keep that one.

Done when

  • Your hook is under 15 seconds
  • It starts with a person or a problem
  • It leads straight into your conflict beat

Chapter 4

Record it well

A good story recorded badly is a bad demo. If people have to strain to hear you, they stop listening. Every fix in this chapter needs only a phone or laptop and about fifteen minutes.

What the research says

Sound quality changes whether people believe you. In a 2018 study, researchers played people the same conference talks and radio interviews by scientists, some with clear audio and some with poor audio. With poor audio, listeners judged the scientists as less intelligent and less likeable, and their research as less important. Introducing the scientists’ credentials first made no difference. The researchers’ advice: when you’re recorded, make sure the sound is good, because your credibility depends on it.

The room

  • Choose a small room with soft things in it: a bedroom with a bed, curtains and a rug is ideal. Avoid kitchens, bathrooms and empty rooms, which echo.
  • Turn off fans and air conditioning, close the windows, and put your phone on silent.
  • Do the clap test: clap once. If you hear the clap ring afterwards, the room echoes. Add a blanket or move rooms.

The microphone

Option When to use it
Wired earbuds with a mic Best for most people; keeps the mic close and steady
Your phone, held 15–20 cm from your mouth Good, if you keep the distance the same throughout
Your laptop’s built-in mic Last resort; it picks up the room and the keyboard

Whichever you use, don’t touch or move the mic while you talk.

The 10-second test

Before every full take, record ten seconds and play it back on headphones. Listen for four problems:

  1. Echo: your voice sounds like it’s in a hallway.
  2. Hum: a steady background noise from a fan or fridge.
  3. Distortion: loud words crackle. Move the mic further away or speak more softly.
  4. Distance: you sound far away. Move the mic closer.

Fix one thing, then test again.

Delivery

  • Speak from bullet points, not a script you read. Reading flattens your voice.
  • Slow down. Two minutes at an easy pace is about 260 words. If your script is longer, cut words; don’t speed up.
  • Pause between beats. A one-second pause tells the viewer a new part is starting.
  • Record one take per beat, then join them. If you stumble, redo just that beat.

Screen and camera

  • Record your screen at 1080p, with notifications turned off and other tabs closed.
  • Zoom your browser in so text is readable on a phone.
  • If you appear on camera, keep it at eye level with the light in front of you, not behind.

Finishing

  • No music under your voice. If you want music, keep it to the opening and closing seconds.
  • Turn on captions and correct any names they get wrong.
  • Name your file exactly: Firstname Lastname – Project Name – Demo.

Exercise

  1. Do the clap test in two rooms and choose the better one.
  2. Record one full take.
  3. Listen back once on headphones, fix the one problem you hear most, and record again.

Done when

  • Every word is clear on the first listen, without captions
  • Your video runs two minutes or less
  • Your file is named correctly

Putting it together

Fill in this one-page beat sheet before you record. It brings all four chapters into one plan, and it is what reviewers will check your demo against.

The two-minute beat sheet

Time Beat What to show What to say
0:00–0:15 Hook and context One person in one moment A moment, a question, a surprising fact or a “what if” (Chapter 3). Your name and your product’s name come after.
0:15–0:35 Conflict Why it’s hard for them today, with one sourced number “Today they have to ___. [Source] found ___, which tells us ___.”
0:35–1:20 Climax Your product, following that person through one task “Here is what ___ does now.” Show only the screens this task needs.
1:20–1:40 Evidence What testing changed “We assumed ___. Testers told us ___. So we changed ___.”
1:40–2:00 Closure What changes for them, and your ask “Now ___ can ___. Next, we need ___ to try it.”

A worked example

Here is the beat sheet filled in for Borrow Box, a made-up project, so you can see how the parts fit together. Amara, Joe and the testing details are invented for this example.

Time Beat What is said
0:00–0:15 Hook and context “Amara arrived at midnight to an empty flat. No pot, no lamp, no one to ask.”
0:15–0:35 Conflict “Like many new students, she can’t afford to buy everything in her first week. I’m Alex, and this is Borrow Box.” (In a real demo, add one sourced number here.)
0:35–1:20 Climax “Borrow Box shows Amara three neighbours with a pot to lend. She picks Joe, two streets away, and taps Borrow. Joe replies: Saturday morning.”
1:20–1:40 Evidence “We assumed students wanted cheap things to buy. Testers told us they only needed them for a few weeks. So we lend instead of sell.”
1:40–2:00 Closure “On Saturday, Amara cooks her first meal at home. We’d like one neighbourhood to try Borrow Box this month.”

Read aloud, that is about 140 words: comfortably inside two minutes, with time for the demo itself.

Before you submit

Check the story:

  • It runs two minutes or less
  • It opens with a hook, not your name or your app
  • One person carries the story from start to finish, rather than a tour of screens
  • It shows what testing changed
  • Every number has a source on screen
  • It ends with a clear ask

Check the sound and the file:

  • Recorded in a quiet room, with the mic close
  • Played back once on headphones, every word is clear without captions
  • Captions on, with names corrected
  • File named Firstname Lastname – Project Name – Demo
